## The proposition, tested

The proposition was: the Netherlands shows that interest-servicing capacity and terminal principal risk can be regulated separately, whereas the current French HCSF interpretation brings the terminal principal into the affordability calculation.

The second half is the published French reading and is unchanged. The HCSF FAQ of 29 January 2024, question 22, says an in fine loan is in scope and that the final principal enters the last year’s charges, which will very probably exceed the 35% effort rate. The FAQ says it has no normative force. That remains the binding published reading for scale.

The first half is not correct as stated.

For a new mortgage, the Dutch ministerial rule does not test the contractual interest-only payment. Article 3(2) of the Tijdelijke regeling hypothecair krediet, in force from 1 January 2026, says that the financing burden is, regardless of the form of the mortgage credit, the calculated gross annual interest and repayment of an annuity over thirty years, or over the shorter contractual term if the loan is shorter than thirty years. A twenty-year interest-only contract is therefore tested, by default, as a twenty-year annuity of interest and principal. Article 3(3) lets the provider use the thirty-year annuity for a shorter loan if that is responsible given the contract and the consumer’s circumstances. It does not let the provider test the interest bill alone.

The balloon is not dropped into year 20, as in the HCSF FAQ. It is also not ignored. Capacity is calculated as if the whole sum were being amortised from the start. An interest-only contract does not increase borrowing capacity relative to an annuity of the same principal, term, and test rate.

Terminal principal risk is then controlled by other instruments, none of which is a statutory duty to file an exit strategy at origination: a 100% consolidated loan-to-value in the same regulation, a 50% cap on the non-amortising slice in an industry code, the 2013 tax condition that new owner-occupied debt must amortise to keep interest deductible, and AFM/DNB supervision of the legacy book. The separation is real. It is not “test the interest, and deal with the principal later.”

## 1. Current affordability treatment

Source status: ministerial regulation, binding on mortgage credit providers under the Wft conduct framework. Text: Tijdelijke regeling hypothecair krediet, BWBR0032503, consolidated text in force 1 January 2026, consulted on wetten.nl. Legal basis: article 115(3) of the Besluit Gedragstoezicht financiële ondernemingen Wft.

What the test uses:

- Not the contractual interest-only instalment.
- A notional annuity of interest and principal (article 3(2)).
- Compared with an allowed burden equal to test income times a financing-load percentage from Bijlage 1 (article 3(5)). The percentage depends on income and on the test-rate band. There is a pre-AOW table and a post-AOW table. Bijlage 2, the simplified percentages, lapsed on 1 January 2016.
- A test rate equal to the offered rate when the fixed period is at least ten years. If the fixed period is shorter than ten years, the test rate is the AFM-published weighted average of 10-year annuity rates, with a floor of 5%, or the offered rate if higher (article 3(9)).
- Other financial obligations added to the burden or deducted from the allowed burden (article 3(12)).
- If the borrower reaches AOW age within ten years, test income must also reflect expected pension income (article 2(5)).

Nibud’s 2026 advice to the ministries explains why the regulation is built on the first year of a thirty-year annuity: that is the year of the lowest net payment on a deductible annuity, and Nibud treats the later rise as gradual enough, given wage growth, to use that first year. Nibud also calculates separate financing-load percentages for parts whose interest is not deductible, including non-annuity loans outside the tax transitional regime. Those “box 3” percentages are in the Nibud advice. They are not in the consolidated regulation text consulted here, which has no box 3 table. This note therefore treats the annuity burden against Bijlage 1 as the binding rule, and the box 3 table as Nibud advice that lenders may apply more strictly. It does not claim that every lender uses a second statutory table.

### Worked example

Assumptions fixed by the regulation, not by a lender quote. Loan €100,000. Contractual interest 4%, fixed for the whole 20-year term, so the test rate is the offered 4%, not the 5% floor. Monthly annuity in arrears, as article 3(4) requires. Property value €300,000 to €500,000, so this advance is 33% to 20% of value.

| Burden tested | Annual amount | Monthly amount |
|---|---:|---:|
| Contractual interest only | €4,000 | €333 |
| Notional 30-year annuity at 4% (only if article 3(3) is judged responsible) | €5,729 | €477 |
| Notional 20-year annuity at 4% (the default, because the term is under 30 years) | €7,272 | €606 |

The 20-year notional annuity is about 1.8 times the contractual interest. The 30-year notional annuity is about 1.4 times the contractual interest.

The financing-load percentage is not a flat rate, so there is no single minimum income. One cell, read from Bijlage 1, table 1, income €60,000, test-rate band 3.501–4.000%: 22.6%. Allowed burden about €13,560. That cell covers €7,272, so this €100,000 loan is not income-constrained at that income. It is also inside a 50% interest-only slice and inside the 100% consolidated loan-to-value. The same €100,000 plus the final principal in one French year is about €104,000 against €60,000, an effort rate far above 35%.

A larger case shows where the Dutch income test binds even though the interest bill would not. An interest-only slice of €200,000 is 50% of a €400,000 property. At 4% and 20 years the notional annuity is about €14,544. At the same €60,000 cell the allowed burden is about €13,560, so the default test fails. The contractual interest would be €8,000 and would pass that cell. A thirty-year notional annuity of the same €200,000 is about €11,458 and would pass the cell if the provider used article 3(3). The French last-year reading would include the €200,000 principal and would fail.

Arithmetic under article 3. Not a bank offer. Other debts, pension income within ten years of AOW, a fixed period under ten years, and any lender use of Nibud’s non-deductible percentages would all change the result.

## 2. What the 50% limit is

It is not in the Tijdelijke regeling. Article 5 of that regulation caps the mortgage at 100% of property value, or 106% when energy measures are financed. That is a consolidated loan-to-value. It is not an interest-only cap.

The 50% figure is an industry norm. The AFM states that, since 2011, the sector agreed to originate interest-only only up to 50% of the property value. The Gedragscode Hypothecaire Financieringen (NVB / Contactorgaan Hypothecair Financiers; 2020 text as cited by the code’s own explanatory material) says that at most 50% of market value at origination may have no contractual repayment or wealth-build schedule. The rest must amortise or be covered by wealth build. “Aflossingsvrij” in that code still means the principal is due at the end of the term and on sale. The borrower remains responsible for how.

Status: industry code, treated by the AFM as the sector norm since 2011, plus supervisory expectation. Not a statute, not a tax rule, and not an NHG monopoly. NHG copies the 50% figure into its own conditions (below). A lender outside the code is not automatically bound by the code’s text. This note does not call the 50% rule universal law.

It is 50% of market value at the origination being assessed, not a permanent cap on the stock. An existing interest-only balance can exceed 50%. The code’s stated exceptions include refinancing without an increase, including costs, while the household stays in the home, and counting the existing financing when there is a second mortgage or an increase. Oversluiting above 50% often forces conversion of the excess; that is the code’s logic, not a finding that every legacy loan has been converted. DNB’s supervisory material treats a large legacy book whose repayment source is the house, including loans that are not inside a 50% current loan-to-value.

Three different limits, which France 2040 must not collapse:

| Limit | Dutch instrument | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| Interest-only slice | Industry code, 50% of market value at origination; NHG repeats it | How much of the value may lack a repayment schedule |
| Consolidated mortgage | TRHK article 5, 100% (106% with energy works) | All mortgage debt against value |
| Income capacity | TRHK article 3, notional annuity against Bijlage 1 | Income, not the interest-only contract |

## 3. Terminal principal risk

There is no statutory requirement, in the regulation read here, that the borrower present a named exit strategy at origination.

What exists instead:

- The code states that the principal is due at maturity and on sale, and that the borrower is responsible for repayment. Sale, savings, voluntary amortisation, and refinancing are possible means. They are not a menu the regulation scores one by one.
- Article 3(2) already withholds the income capacity that a pure interest test would grant.
- Article 2(5) pulls expected pension income into the test when AOW is within ten years.
- DNB Open Boek Toezicht, “Aflossingsvrije hypotheken”, draws the prudential distinction directly. For an amortising loan, income is normally the primary repayment source and sale is a fallback. For an interest-only loan, the property value is usually the primary repayment source, and that value can fall. Balances are widely extended or refinanced, so the debt stays high. Future affordability is uncertain at retirement, unemployment, disability, a rate rise, or the loss of deductibility. DNB’s expectation of institutions: adequate collateral information, a view of future affordability, a balance between the interest-only volume and capital, and pricing and balance-sheet valuation that reflect the extra risk. That is supervision, shared with the AFM and, for significant banks, the ECB. It is not an origination statute.
- The AFM’s public line, including its 2026 update of the interest-only page, is that customers should be able to refinance or fully repay at the end of the economic term, or sell without a residual debt, and carry the payments through retirement and the end of deductibility. From 2018, providers run a structured approach: contact, affordability tests, solutions. Risk segmentation uses loan-to-value toward the end of the term and the years left until maturity, retirement, or loss of deductibility.

Older AFM material recorded that more than half of Dutch mortgage debt was interest-only and that a large share of households might lack the savings to repay in full. A pilot found future affordability under pressure for a substantial minority of customers. France 2040 already cites DNB, 3 March 2026: interest-only still almost 40% of mortgage debt. This note does not replace that figure with a later secondary estimate.

## 4. Existing mortgage plus a further interest-only slice

Dutch distribution of an extra slice is not the French junior-mortgage finding.

Same lender. The ordinary product is an extra loan part (leningdeel) under the existing mortgage relationship, often one right of mortgage rather than a separately registered second rank. On an increase, the code counts the existing financing. The interest-only slice remains capped at 50% of value at that origination, subject to the refinancing exception that preserves an existing interest-only share when principal does not rise. The new income test applies to the financing burden, and other obligations count under article 3(12). The consolidated loan-to-value still faces article 5.

NHG. Voorwaarden en normen 2026-1, C.6.7.1: an interest-only NHG loan is allowed only if the interest-only part relates to existing eigenwoningschuld under the tax transitional rule (Wet IB 2001, article 10bis.1) and is not larger than 50% of market value. It is refused for specified leasehold or buyer-support structures that add wealth risk. NHG’s financing-last chapter applies article 3 of the Tijdelijke regeling and, as a rule, does not let the provider use the regulation’s exceptions. An additional NHG loan is with the same lender. That is an NHG condition, not a rule for every Dutch mortgage.

Different lender. A second mortgage at another bank is legally possible. This note does not find a regulation that makes it a standard interoperable product, and it does not adopt broker statements about a 75% inscription cap or a routine consent requirement. Those are lender-practice claims until read in a regulatory text. Tax: a post-2012 increase that does not itself amortise is not deductible owner-occupied debt. The grandfathered amount can survive a refinance or a move only if the new debt does not exceed it.

Comparison with France. Code civil article 2418 lets a later mortgage take rank from its own registration, without a general statutory duty to refinance the first loan or to obtain the first lender’s consent merely to create the junior lien. Covenants, the secured amount, and further draws still have to be read. The Dutch constraint on a further interest-only slice is not a missing second rank. It is the combination of same-lender loan parts, the 50% code, the annuity income test, the 100% consolidated loan-to-value, and the tax condition.

## 6. Why interest-only was restricted rather than prohibited

These are different acts. They are often collapsed in secondary commentary.

Prudential and macroprudential. The 100% loan-to-value, capital and pricing expectations, and the supervisory treatment of a stock whose repayment depends on house prices and refinancing. DNB does not describe a prohibition.

Affordability. The 2013 ministerial regulation made the Nibud income test binding for new credit, on a notional annuity regardless of form. That limits how large an interest-only loan can be. It does not ban the form.

Loan-to-value. Article 5 is the statutory consolidated cap. The 50% slice is the industry cap on the non-amortising part at origination.

Tax. Kamerstuk 33405, nr. 3, and the 2013 implementing measure: for interest on owner-occupied debt to stay deductible, the contract must repay at least on an annuity path, in full within at most 360 months. Linear repayment qualifies if the balance stays at or below that path. Debt that was already eigenwoningschuld on 31 December 2012 is grandfathered. A later increase is deductible only if that increase itself amortises. A new interest-only loan can still be made; the non-qualifying part is box 3 debt and the interest is not deductible. Converting a qualifying loan to interest-only drops that part out of the deduction. The AFM states that this tax change reduced new interest-only origination. Loss of deductibility is not a ban on lending.

Legacy book. Pre-2013 interest-only debt was not called in. Supervision shifted to contact, future affordability, collateral, and solutions, because the stock remains large and its primary repayment source is the property.

## What this implies for France 2040

The Dutch precedent does not justify testing only the interest payment. An interest-only contract can coexist with an affordability calculation that notionally amortises the principal. That is a third approach, between two weaker ones.

(a) Testing only the contractual interest understates principal risk. In the worked example the household pays €333 a month. An income test that stops there treats €4,000 a year as the whole burden of a €100,000 debt.

(b) Putting the entire balloon into the final year’s affordability ratio, which is the published HCSF FAQ reading, makes a long in-fine loan mechanically difficult. The same €100,000 produces a last-year charge of about €104,000.

The Dutch rule does neither. Article 3(2) puts principal into the affordability test as a notional annuity, while the contract can still charge only interest. Other controls then sit beside that test: the non-amortising share, consolidated loan-to-value, tax, and supervision of the residual at maturity. The household keeps the cash-flow benefit. It does not receive €100,000 merely because it can pay €333 a month. Underwriting uses a burden closer to €606 a month on a 20-year annuity, or €477 a month if the provider is allowed the 30-year annuity.

That is a different question to put to the HCSF, if it is put at all. Not “please exclude the €100,000 principal from the 35% effort rate.” Rather: could an interest-only advance secured on a home already owned be tested on an equivalent annualised principal capacity, together with a cap on the non-amortising tranche and a separate consolidated loan-to-value? The first question asks the authority to ignore a risk. The second asks whether the same risk can be measured another way. This note does not recommend that France adopt the second question. It is a candidate for testing. Nothing here establishes that the HCSF would accept it.

### Three variables, kept separate

A single “40%” or “50%” does not describe the constraint. Every subsequent France 2040 text needs three numbers.

1. Non-amortising tranche loan-to-value: the new interest-only advance divided by property value.
2. Consolidated household mortgage loan-to-value: existing mortgage plus the new advance, divided by property value.
3. Income-based borrowing capacity: how much debt the household can carry under a stated affordability rule. A notional amortisation test is one candidate. It is not implied by either loan-to-value.

A €500,000 house with €100,000 outstanding and a new €200,000 advance has a tranche loan-to-value of 40% and a consolidated loan-to-value of 60%. Neither number says whether the household can afford the debt. National housing equity, the stock behind EC-01, is collateral capacity. It is not household origination capacity. The Dutch rule is a concrete regulatory model of that distinction. It is not a revision of EC-01. Propagation into that test is later work.

Rounded to the nearest €100. The unrounded 35% incomes are €297,143, €11,429, €20,776, €16,369 and €18,097.

Two results follow directly.

Against the HCSF FAQ, the notional annuity is what would make an ordinary income able to support a long interest-only contract. A household on €40,000 can carry a last-year balloon of about €13,500. The same income, at 35% of a 20-year notional annuity, can carry about €192,500 of principal. The FAQ treatment does not support a housing-equity programme at ordinary incomes. A notional test would.

Against contractual-interest affordability, the same notional test cuts principal. At a common 35% and at 4%, income that supports €100 of interest-only principal supports about €55 on a 20-year notional annuity, about €63 on a 25-year annuity, and about €70 on a 30-year notional annuity. The Dutch table is tighter still, because the financing-load percentage at these incomes is near 21–23%, not 35%. €100,000 at the 20-year annuity clears Bijlage 1 from €33,000, not from the €20,800 that a 35% test would require.

The cash-flow benefit survives the stricter test, and only for households who pass it. On €100,000 they pay €333 a month and were underwritten at €606. The gap against a 20-year amortising payment is about €273 a month. That gap is retained cash. It is not extra principal. Under a 20-year notional test at the same rate and the same effort rate, the maximum principal equals the maximum principal of an ordinary 20-year amortising loan.

The last row is the age point in the Dutch rule. If retirement falls within ten years, article 2(5) requires expected pension income in the test. The €500,000 case is the one that keeps the three variables apart. Tranche 40%. Consolidated 60%. Income capacity is a third fact: about €41,600 at 35% of a 20-year notional annuity on the new advance alone, and about €62,300 if the outstanding €100,000 is also underwritten as a 20-year annuity. A reader who stops at “40%” has not described this household.

### Consequence for the €700bn path

The path is not changed. €700bn stays a scenario input. This arithmetic does not estimate how many French households would pass.

It does change what would count as evidence for that scale.

The original attractiveness of the Pacte is that interest-only produces more immediate household cash flow than amortising credit. A Dutch-style test keeps that cash flow for households who qualify, and refuses the extra principal that an interest-only payment test would have allowed. Eligibility is then the eligibility of amortising credit of the same term, rate, and effort rate. The programme can still differ from an ordinary mortgage in the monthly payment. It cannot, on income grounds, be larger than the principal those same households could have borrowed on an amortising loan.

Relative to a test that used only the interest bill, a 20-year notional annuity at the same 35% and 4% leaves about 55% of the principal. That is the shrink. It is a shrink against an affordability rule the Netherlands does not use and the HCSF FAQ does not use. It is not a measured cut of the €700bn.

Relative to the FAQ, the notional test is what would make household origination of a long in-fine advance possible at all. The FAQ income for a €100,000 balloon is about €297,000. That rule, applied as published, does not leave a €500–700bn in-fine programme standing.

The unresolved question is whether income capacity under a notional test, intersected with the tranche cap, the consolidated cap, existing debt service, and retirement income, still covers a stock of that size. Collateral said yes at 40% of housing equity. Income has not been asked. The layers between the national stock and an actual loan are collateral, security, existing debt, tranche loan-to-value, consolidated loan-to-value, income capacity, bank underwriting, funding, household take-up, and origination. This note adds a specified income layer. It does not fill it.

As an illustration only, not a result: if €700bn were already known to be originatable as 4% 20-year interest-only debt, contractual interest would be €28bn a year and the 20-year notional annuity would be about €51bn a year. The cash-flow wedge would be about €23bn a year. That sentence assumes the stock. It does not find the stock. No Dutch percentage, 50% cap, or 100% consolidated loan-to-value is imported into the path.

### Candidate architecture, not a proposal

The combination to test, and not yet to adopt:

- contractual interest-only cash flow;
- a notional amortising affordability test;
- a conservative cap on the non-amortising tranche, kept separate from;
- a consolidated loan-to-value;
- valuation;
- a retirement and terminal-risk assessment;
- disclosure and reporting.

Asking the HCSF to apply a notional annuity instead of the last-year balloon would be an HCSF decision. The tranche cap, the consolidated cap, valuation, and disclosure can be specified privately and still would not, under the published FAQ, stop the balloon from entering the last year’s charges. Banks’ ability to fund and price the product is untouched. So is household take-up.

### Next test

Quantify household-level origination capacity before using aggregate housing equity as evidence of programme scale. Report the filters separately. Do not collapse them into one eligible stock, and do not replace €700bn with the output.

For owner-occupiers, joint on income, age, property value, and outstanding mortgage:

1. Tranche room: min(chosen tranche cap × value, any product maximum). Caps at 30 / 40 / 50% of value, reported separately.
2. Consolidated room: min(chosen consolidated cap × value − existing mortgage, tranche room). The consolidated cap is a scenario choice. The Dutch 100% is not the default.
3. Income room, five ways, on the same households: FAQ balloon; contractual interest; 20-year notional annuity; 30-year notional annuity; ordinary amortising credit at the French maturity used for that case. Include the existing loan’s actual debt service. State the rate and whether insurance is in the charge.
4. Retirement room: where the term crosses retirement, or retirement falls within ten years, repeat the income room on expected pension income rather than current earnings.
5. Originatable advance: the minimum of tranche room, consolidated room, and the income room under the rule being tested.

The output that matters is the distribution of that minimum, and the national sum under each income rule. A large collateral sum and a much smaller income-constrained sum are different findings. Either result leaves EC-01, EC-05, and the €700bn path open until that measurement is done and accepted.

### Verdict

Established: France and the Netherlands both permit interest-only structures. The Netherlands demonstrates that an interest-only contractual cash flow can coexist with an affordability calculation that notionally amortises principal. France’s published HCSF reading instead places the final principal in the last year’s charges. Security for a separate French mortgage was already established in the standardisation note and is not reopened here.

Not established: that France should adopt the Dutch calculation; that the HCSF would accept a notional annuity in place of the balloon; that French banks could fund and price the resulting product attractively; or that enough French households would qualify to sustain the €500–700bn path.

Next test: quantify household-level origination capacity under those alternative affordability treatments before using aggregate housing equity as evidence for the scale of the programme.

## Tax and the use of retained liquidity

This section tests one proposition. Withdrawing mortgage-interest deductibility from new non-amortising debt increases the after-tax cost of leaving principal outstanding, and therefore raises the opportunity cost of keeping that principal rather than repaying it.

The proposition is right as a price effect. It is not evidence that Dutch households invest or save the payment difference rather than consume it. France 2040 should not say that the Dutch tax system pushes interest-only proceeds toward investment.

### The price effect

On the worked example, contractual interest is €4,000 a year, €333 a month. While that interest is deductible, the household’s net cost is €4,000 times one minus the deduction rate. In 2026 the Belastingdienst caps that rate at 37.56% for income in the top bracket (the cap equals the second-bracket rate; Wet IB 2001, articles 2.10 and 2.10a). At that cap the tax saving is about €1,500 a year and the net interest bill is about €2,500, or €208 a month. A household taxed in a lower bracket deducts at a lower rate, so the saving is smaller.

Withdrawing the deduction puts the gross €4,000 back on the household. The cost of keeping €100,000 outstanding rises by about 1.5 percentage points at the 2026 cap, before any box 3 effect. Withdrawing the deduction raises the opportunity cost of keeping principal outstanding. For an investment, this raises the return required to justify financing it rather than repaying debt. For consumption or transfers, it raises the price of choosing present use over deleveraging.

Two offsets belong in the price, and neither turns it into an investment mandate.

Box 3. New interest-only owner-occupied debt that fails the amortisation test is not eigenwoningschuld. The interest is not deductible in box 1. The principal is a box 3 liability, so it can reduce taxable net wealth for households above the box 3 threshold. That relief offsets part of the lost deduction. It is a reason the hurdle does not rise by the full box 1 amount, and it is larger for households that already hold taxable financial wealth. This note does not put a 2026 box 3 rate on the €1,500. The direction is enough: the offset is a wealth-tax interaction, not a test of what the household buys.

Purpose of the borrowed sum. The Belastingdienst grants the deduction only if the loan is used to buy the home, or to improve or maintain it, or to buy out a ground lease, and, for new debt, only if the contract amortises within 30 years. A loan part used for a car or furniture is refused the deduction even when the rest of the mortgage amortises. Lenders already book that part as a consumptive, non-deductible slice. The purpose rule conditions the subsidy on the use of the principal. It does not govern the monthly gap between an annuity and an interest-only payment.

### What households actually did

The demonstrated response to the 2013 rule is a change of contract, not a change in how interest-only borrowers invest the gap.

CPB, Besparingen van Nederlandse huishoudens, 9 July 2024. After the deduction was limited to annuity and linear loans that repay within 30 years, interest-only new borrowing became much less attractive. The share of households that repay a mortgage rose from 25% in 2007 to more than 50% in 2019. The household saving rate rose from −3% in 2005 to about 2%. The extra saving is mortgage repayment. Liquid buffers, the balances a household can spend in a shock, were rebuilt less than that repayment. CPB notes the same saving-rate rise in Denmark and Sweden, where policy also pushed contractual repayment. Younger cohorts repay more than earlier ones. The low post-2008 mortgage rate and higher house prices are named alongside the tax rule. The paper does not measure a shift from consumption into business investment among households who kept an interest-only loan.

The pre-2013 stock mixed three things. Pure interest-only loans had no repayment schedule. Savings, life and investment mortgages paired a non-amortising loan with a pledged account (kapitaalverzekering eigen woning, spaarrekening eigen woning, beleggingsrecht eigen woning). Those accounts were blocked for repayment of the home loan and, if the conditions were met, kept out of box 3. That did channel money toward a repayment asset. It did so by contract and by a tax exemption for a blocked balance. It is not evidence about an unconstrained household choosing investment over consumption. DNB’s 2015 loan-level study found that about 60% of outstanding debt was interest-only, often combined with an amortising part or a pledged account, and that voluntary repayments were about 2–3% of mortgage debt in 2013–2014. Voluntary repayment is repayment, not a portfolio of productive assets. Mastrogiacomo’s simulations find that voluntary repayment and pledged accounts matter, and that many households still will not have saved enough outside the house to repay in full at maturity. Home equity, not a financial investment account, is what keeps the credit risk down in those simulations.

The direct survey of saving by interest-only owners points the other way. Lennartz, Vrieselaar and Blanken, ESB, 24 September 2018, using a Nibud survey of January 2018 commissioned by Rabobank (2,084 respondents, of whom 248 had a fully interest-only mortgage and 263 a partial one). Owners with an interest-only mortgage less often intended to set money aside: 42%, against 64% for owners with a repaying mortgage. Once age, income and other characteristics are held constant, having an interest-only mortgage has no statistically significant effect on saving intentions or on reported saving behaviour. They save less inside the house, because they do not repay. Outside the house they do not save more. The authors’ reading is selection and capacity: before the deduction was tightened, households that preferred not to save could take interest-only cheaply; many of those owners are older and on lower incomes; others treat housing equity as the buffer and plan to refinance or sell. EenVandaag (2017) and Vereniging Eigen Huis (2018), as cited there, found that a majority of interest-only owners did not plan to repay in full from savings.

No source read for this note shows that the monthly gap was invested in firms, equipment, or financial assets rather than consumed. The absence is the finding. The behavioural effect that is demonstrated is that new borrowers switched toward contracts that force repayment, and that aggregate saving rose in that form.

### What this does and does not say for France 2040

Tax neutrality — the same treatment whether the household amortises or not, and whether the proceeds are consumed or invested — would stop subsidising the choice to leave principal outstanding. For an investment, the gross borrowing rate, net of any general wealth-tax treatment of the debt, is the return required to justify financing it rather than repaying. For consumption or a transfer, it is the price of choosing present use over deleveraging. Tax neutrality does not select investment and does not restrict the use of the money.

The Dutch post-2013 reform is not a model for that design. It kept the subsidy on condition that the household amortise: income, then principal repayment, then housing equity. The mechanism under study runs the other way: income, then interest, then retained cash flow, then a household choice among consumption, renovation, investment and transfer. A fiscal advantage for amortising that debt would push cash back into the house and work against the mechanism. A fiscal advantage limited to qualifying productive uses would be a different instrument, with definitions, eligibility, verification and a fiscal cost.

The lesson to keep is narrower. Do not subsidise the optionality. An interest-only contract gives the household control of cash that amortisation would have put into the house automatically. The benefit is that liquidity. The household then decides whether spending, investing, renovating or transferring €10,000 is worth the financing cost. No ministry has to rank those uses.

A deduction, credit, or exemption reserved for qualifying productive uses is a different instrument. It is closer to the Dutch purpose condition than to the 2013 amortisation condition. The household can still spend the money. The subsidy is paid only when the use qualifies. That is a restriction of the tax benefit, defined in law, even though it is not a ban on the expenditure. The Dutch record does not show that this kind of condition, applied to the monthly interest-only gap, moves spending into investment. The condition that did move behaviour withdrew the subsidy unless the contract itself amortised, which removes the cash-flow benefit for anyone who wants the subsidy.

The blocked savings mortgage is the Dutch device that actually tied the gap to an asset. It required the asset to be pledged to repayment of the home loan and gave that asset a tax exemption. Copying it would legally restrict the use of that cash. It is not a model of free use plus a gentle incentive.

Phrase the Dutch tax fact this narrowly: withdrawing deductibility from new non-amortising debt raises the opportunity cost of retaining principal. For an investment, that raises the required return. For consumption or a transfer, it raises the price of present use over deleveraging. It does not establish that households invest the liquidity. Changing the relative price changed the financing structure. It did not, on the evidence, change the productive allocation of the liberated cash.

Five choices stay separate after this note: creating the liquidity, the income test that decides how much can be borrowed, the collateral limits that keep the balance sheet resilient, the tax treatment, and what the household does with the funds. National housing equity is only the first. This note does not belong in the €700bn path, and it does not change the HCSF question above. The test that can reduce or support that path is the household origination-capacity measurement already specified. It has not been run.

## What France should not copy

- A legacy interest-only stock on the Dutch scale, still close to 40% of mortgage debt in 2026, whose primary repayment source is the house and whose balances are commonly extended.
- Treating sale or refinancing as the planned way out. The French note already lists that as a reason to stop.
- The pre-2013 tax preference that made non-amortising owner-occupied debt cheap, followed by a rule that withdrew the preference for new debt and left the old book in place. Do not describe that rule as evidence that interest-only proceeds were invested. The demonstrated response is contractual repayment. The survey evidence does not show extra saving outside the house.
- A 100% consolidated loan-to-value.
- The assumption that same-lender loan parts are a substitute for a separately registered second mortgage. France’s security finding is the broader one. The Dutch market does not show a routine different-lender second mortgage as the mass product.
- Any Dutch parameter inside the 700 Md€ path.
