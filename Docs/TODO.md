## Order of work

1. **Freeze the thesis.** Done as a decision, above. V2 is the reference. Keep the Decision Log so the argument does not drift.
2. **Build the quantitative model.** Largest piece of work. A 2027–2040 model linking mortgage-equity extraction → household use of funds → domestic demand, imports and savings → investment and supply → employment → nominal and real GDP → tax receipts → expenditure → deficit → public debt. Then Base / Low / High / Failure. The €500–700bn should become an output, or a tested scenario, not an assertion. Sketch: `08_Pacte_Modele_Macro.xlsx` and the public file. v0.1 still multiplies credit by 0.65 then by 0.35. That product stays operational and is labelled an untested input. It is not changed. The `Conversion probes` sheet tests 25 / 50 / 75 percent conversion of credit into additional spending only. The French-production share and the minimum transmission stay blank. The numerical success test is not defined until later red-team results exist. See `10_RedTeam_02_Credit_to_Spend.md`.
3. **Build the consolidated French balance sheet.** Public debt and assets; household financial and real-estate assets and debt; corporate balance sheet; banking system; external position. The question: is France short of wealth, or is part of the problem where leverage and assets sit? First cut, from Insee Première n° 2081, is in `09_RedTeam_01_Equity_Reservoir.md`: household net wealth €14 953bn, government net wealth €690bn, government net financial wealth −€2 132bn. Corporate and banking detail, and a stock-flow link to the model, are still open.
4. **Household distribution.** Collateral test survived: `09_RedTeam_01_Equity_Reservoir.md`. €700bn is about 31% of the theoretical residual to a 40% consolidated LTV (€2,251bn). Outright ownership is 37.4% of households and 81.9% among couples aged 65 or over. Do not change the €700bn path. Next measurement reports four separate results at 30 / 40 / 50% LTV: collateral ceiling, debt-service ceiling, regulatory/product ceiling, take-up. A servicing rule that cuts a large collateral stock down to €450bn is a different finding from a distribution that only supports €450bn. Borrower-level capacity and regulatory compatibility stay unresolved.
5. **International precedents.** UK equity release and lifetime mortgages, US HELOC and cash-out refinancing, Australia, and others remain for the Evidence Book. The Netherlands is opened inside Red Team 05 as a case where policy moved away from new interest-only debt, not as a template. Denmark and Switzerland are not read yet.
6. **French hypothèque rechargeable, 2006–2014.** Why it did not scale: demand, distribution, regulation, pricing, consumer protection, banking incentives, or the wrong product. V2, section XXI, only notes that it was introduced in 2006 and repealed for households in 2014. No deep dive.
7. **Banking plumbing.** Open as Red Team 05: `13_RedTeam_05_Bank_Funding.md`. France first. Capital, liquidity, duration and refinancing are not filled. Do not change the product if the frozen path creates a funding problem.
8. **Fiscal side and the Pacte Spread.** A credible baseline without the Pacte: nominal GDP, public spending, tax receipts, interest, primary balance, debt/GDP. Then the overlay. The question is how much fiscal consolidation becomes possible without an equivalent demand shock. The Spread is defined. It is not yet quantified on a full baseline.
9. **Housing, treated as a central objection.** More borrowing capacity for existing owners can make housing less affordable for younger households. Need construction elasticity, land constraints, price-to-income ratios, first-time buyers, renters, downsizing. The Pacte needs a housing compact, not a paragraph of safeguards. First supply fact is in `11_RedTeam_03_Spending_to_Output.md`: French urban housing supply elasticity is about 0.5, so extra housing demand shows up in prices unless supply moves with it. Marker from Red Team 05, not imported into that test: the Commission’s 2024 Netherlands in-depth review says the supply of new dwellings remains very inelastic to price changes. The compact itself is not written.
10. **Four contracts, specified.** Patrimonial and financial; fiscal; productive; employment and security of income. For each: obligations, metrics, thresholds, governance, and what happens if the other side is not delivered. Sketch only: `05_Pacte_Contrat_Social.docx`.
11. **Red-team document.** “25 reasons why the Pacte du Bilan Français could be a terrible idea.” Housing bubble, regressive transfer, Ricardian effects, low MPC among wealthy owners, imports, inflation, bank concentration, fiscal moral hazard, EU constraints, retirees spending their buffer, political capture, and the rest. Each objection marked evidence, unresolved, or potentially fatal. No advocacy. Not started.
12. **Legal and EU feasibility.** HCSF, ACPR, Banque de France, ECB/SSM, CRR/CRD, consumer-credit and mortgage rules, covered bonds, French succession and mortgage law, EU fiscal framework. Not a treatise. A map of what would make the architecture impossible or materially different. Open in the Decision Log. Not started.
13. **Public model.** The spreadsheet becomes downloadable from France2040.eu. A reader can change LTV, adoption, extraction, MPC, import leakage, real growth, inflation, spending growth, interest rates, and see the thesis hold or fail. Not started. Depends on item 2.
14. **France2040.eu, kept small.** Possible first pages: the hypothesis, the paper, the model, evidence, critique, about. No movement, membership, manifesto, or party branding. It should look like independent economic research. A local shell exists (home, French pact draft, documents, project). It is not this site yet, and it is not deployed.
15. **Three versions of the argument.** A 30–40 page reference paper; a 4–6 page policy brief; a one-page executive note. V2 is the long narrative reference. Paper v1 is shorter than the reference paper described here. No brief and no one-page note yet.

A macroeconomist, a housing economist, a former or current banker, a public-finance specialist, an actuary, a mortgage specialist, a demographer, an EU or regulatory person, a labour economist, and someone hostile to the proposal.
