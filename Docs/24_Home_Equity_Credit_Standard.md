# Standardising home-equity credit

12 October 2026. Research conclusion, not a product approval. Not an épreuve contradictoire.

Working file. English stays here. Public French snapshot: `/documents/standard-credit-logement`, version `2026-10-12-2`. The version `2026-10-12` remains. This note does not close EC-01, EC-05, or the 700 Md€ path, and it does not approve a product.

Checked against primary sources before that publication, and stated there rather than by rewriting this file: the HCSF FAQ of 29 January 2024 says it has no normative force; a 20-year final repayment can sit inside the 25-year maturity cap while the last-year effort rate, on that FAQ’s question 22, very probably exceeds 35%; Banque de France usury categories for Q4 2026 split cash credit from housing credit by article L313-1(1°), not by the whole article; ACPR 2024 states 99% fixed-rate production, 97% of outstanding secured, and mortgages at 27% of outstanding. “Crédit Logement covers over a third” is not repeated on the public page. Code civil article 2416 is the professional rechargeable mortgage. CRR article 108(5)(c) is a prudential condition for treating a cautioned loan as a mortgage exposure, not a general civil ban.

Established here: a separate mortgage can be registered behind an earlier one under existing security law. Still open: cheap, interoperable distribution at scale.

France does not need a new law to let a homeowner take a new mortgage-backed loan while keeping an existing loan. A separate mortgage can be registered behind an earlier one. The harder question is whether banks can routinely offer a long, fixed-rate loan with interest payments and a final principal payment. Under the HCSF’s published interpretation, that final payment enters the affordability test, so a low monthly interest bill does not make an ordinary in fine loan compliant at scale. A voluntary banking standard could solve much of the documentation and collateral problem; broad distribution of the proposed repayment structure would likely also need an HCSF decision. Code de la consommation, art. L313-1; Code civil, art. 2418; HCSF FAQ, questions 20–23.

## 1. What the French precedents show

| Precedent | How it became usable across banks—and its limit as a model |
| --- | --- |
| Ordinary fixed-rate mortgages | There was no single statute creating a uniform bank product. Banks developed underwriting and funding practices around common consumer-credit and security rules, insurance, and guarantees. In 2024, 99% of new housing credit was fixed-rate. The established model is overwhelmingly amortising and assesses repayment capacity primarily from income. It proves that long fixed rates can scale in France, not that a final balloon payment can. ACPR, Le financement de l’habitat en 2024; CCSF, Le modèle français de financement, 2026. |
| PTZ | The state initiated a defined subsidy product. Legislation supplied eligibility and lender compensation; decrees and orders supplied detailed rules; agreements with the state and SGFGAS supplied bank onboarding, declarations, checks and calculation of the tax credit. Banks still decide whether to lend. This is the strongest precedent for a shared operational specification, but its public subsidy and tax machinery are unnecessary for an unsubsidised equity loan. PTZ evaluation; 2011 lender agreement; Service Public. |
| Crédit Logement and other cautions | A shared guarantor made the security decision and its cost available within partner banks’ loan processes, using a mutual guarantee fund and electronic exchanges. Its private-law caution can avoid a new mortgage deed for eligible housing loans. Crédit Logement now covers over a third of the market. This shows how common infrastructure lowers distribution friction; it does not establish that a guarantor will accept unrestricted equity-release loans or a later lien on a property backing an existing guarantee. Crédit Logement history; guarantee process; bank web service. |
| Prêt viager hypothécaire; prêt avance mutation | The 2006 reform created a special equity-release contract. Current law expressly permits periodic payment of interest alone, with principal due on death or an earlier property transfer. The renovation-focused prêt avance mutation has its own statutory, regulatory and, for some loans, tax or guarantee framework. A legal form therefore exists, but it has not produced a broad multi-bank market: a government response recorded fewer than 1,000 viager loans annually from the original distributor before its production stopped in 2019. These are specialised products with different maturity and risk economics from an ordinary fixed-term loan. Code de la consommation, arts. L315-1–2; government response to Parliament. |
| Hypothèque rechargeable | The 2006 reform allowed one registered mortgage to secure later debts, including to another lender, within its original amount and rank. The 2014 consumer law removed that facility for households amid concern that property value could drive excessive consumer borrowing. It survives for professional debts. It is a warning against treating an available legal mechanism as proof of demand, safe underwriting or bank adoption. A fresh second-ranking mortgage is legally distinct from reusing the first mortgage’s rank. Senate legislative report; Code civil, art. 2416. |

A prêt relais offers one further, limited lesson: French banks already lend temporarily against expected property-sale proceeds. The HCSF excludes genuine bridge loans from its origination rule because they are short-lived and linked to a sale. Calling a long-lived equity loan a bridge loan would not reproduce those conditions. HCSF FAQ, question 8.

Lesson: successful scaling has combined a clear contract and consumer journey with repeatable underwriting data, a workable guarantee, predictable capital and funding treatment, and bank systems that can process all of them. PTZ needed legislation because it spent public money; Crédit Logement demonstrates that shared private infrastructure can scale without a new product statute.

## 2. The existing-loan test

An unrestricted advance secured by a new mortgage on a dwelling falls within the consumer mortgage-credit rules even if its proceeds do not buy that dwelling. The lender must rigorously assess solvency; collateral value alone is insufficient. The ordinary mortgage must be made by notarial act and its rank follows registration. Code de la consommation, arts. L313-1 and L313-16; Code civil, arts. 2409 and 2418.

There are three materially different cases:

1. Existing first mortgage, same or different lender. A new, separately registered mortgage can take a later rank without refinancing the old fixed-rate loan. As an inference from the ranking rules, there is no general statutory requirement to replace the first loan or obtain its lender’s consent merely to create a junior mortgage. The actual loan covenants, secured amount, further-draw rights and any proposed change of rank must still be checked. The junior lender bears the loss after senior claims are met. Code civil, arts. 2417–18.
2. Existing loan backed by a caution. A caution is a personal guarantee, not itself a registered first mortgage. A later lender’s new mortgage may therefore be first in the land register. That does not make consent irrelevant: EU prudential treatment of qualifying cautioned residential loans requires the borrower, for loans granted since 2014, to undertake not to create a mortgage without the original lender’s consent. Covered-bond rules contain a related condition. Existing contracts and each guarantor’s terms need review before an interbank consent process can be promised. CRR3, art. 108(4)–(5); CRR, art. 129(1)(e); Crédit Logement on caution versus mortgage.
3. No existing loan. Security and ranking are simpler, but the interest-only repayment and funding questions remain.

For every case, the proposed advance/value cap and consolidated debt/value ratio must be separate tests. A 40% new advance on a home already carrying 40% debt produces 80% consolidated LTV. A second lender needs the old loan’s balance, payment schedule, secured maximum and possible further drawings—not just a land-register search. The register reveals mortgages, while the FICP records incidents rather than all outstanding loans. Information exchange between banks also needs a lawful route under bank-secrecy rules, ordinarily an express customer authorisation for this purpose. DGFIP, mortgage information; Banque de France, FICP; Code monétaire et financier, art. L511-33.

## 3. The binding constraint on an interest-only standard

The HCSF rule normally limits new mortgage credit to 35% debt-service-to-income and 25 years, with a bank-level 20% flexibility allowance largely reserved for purchases of primary residences. Existing debts at other banks count. Its FAQ expressly says that an in fine loan’s final principal repayment counts in the last year’s affordability test and will very likely exceed 35%. A conservative LTV does not override that reading. The allowance can support a bounded experiment where available; it is not a dependable mass-market channel for non-purchase equity advances. A material alteration to this treatment would require HCSF action, not merely an ACPR assurance or an FBF template. HCSF measure; HCSF FAQ, questions 6, 11, 22 and 27.

The cost of a junior loan also cannot be inferred from ordinary first-mortgage rates. CRR3 permits recognition of some enforceable junior liens held by a different bank, subject to conditions; senior claims reduce the junior lender’s preferentially weighted collateral slice. French covered-bond vehicles have their own eligibility limits: for example, sociétés de financement de l’habitat finance loans destined at least in part for residential property, so a general-purpose cash-out loan should not be assumed eligible. The applicable usury category and all-in rate need checking by loan purpose, as the Banque de France distinguishes household cash credit from acquisition credit. These are reasons to test an actual bank funding price before claiming cheap scale. CRR3, arts. 124–125; Code monétaire et financier, art. L513-29; Banque de France, Q4 2026 usury categories.

The CCSF’s 2026 review likewise calls in fine credit marginal for primary residences and does not regard it as a general solution for home purchase. That is evidence against assuming effortless adoption; it does not by itself decide the different use case of a low-LTV advance to an existing homeowner. CCSF, 2026.

## 4. Minimum target architecture

Share a specification, not a credit decision. An FBF-led, open interbank standard could define: the fixed-term product and final-payment disclosure; permitted purposes; separate advance LTV and consolidated LTV; a conservative, independently governed valuation method; an existing-loan balance and secured-amount certificate; lien and caution consent workflows; standard notarial instructions; annual borrower statements; and portfolio reporting of defaults, extensions, sales and final-payment outcomes. Independent valuation and documented solvency already have legal or prudential foundations. Code de la consommation, arts. L313-16 and L313-20; EBA loan-origination guidelines.

Banks should retain their own customer selection, tighter limits, income and exit tests, pricing, funding, guarantee choice and decision to lend. An interbank standard must remain accessible without coordinating prices or exchanging competitively sensitive underwriting plans. European Commission horizontal-cooperation guidelines.

| Actor or instrument | Minimum intervention |
| --- | --- |
| Individual banks and notaries | Under existing law: pilot a separately documented mortgage advance; preserve the old loan; verify title, covenants, total debts, valuation, affordability, security costs and a credible way to repay principal. Use existing mortgage-credit information and reflection requirements. |
| FBF, banks, notaries and guarantors | Agree voluntary data fields, customer-authorised balance exchange, model consent requests, ranking and discharge instructions, comparable cost disclosures and outcome reporting. Guarantors must decide separately whether their existing guarantees tolerate a later lien and whether to underwrite the new product. |
| HCSF; ACPR and Banque de France | HCSF first clarifies how the proposed final payment is treated and, if it wants compliant broad origination, decides whether a narrowly bounded amendment is justified. ACPR then clarifies reporting and supervises application; with banks it validates the legal and prudential treatment of junior liens and cautions. Banque de France checks data and applicable rate-category issues. Clarification alone cannot waive a binding HCSF test. |
| Government and Parliament | No initial product-creation law is identified. Consider legislation only if the pilot shows that a mandatory right to loan data or consent, a new reusable consumer security, a public guarantee or subsidy, or access to a particular funding vehicle is indispensable. A decree or order would then implement the chosen statute or amend rules within an existing delegation. |

## 5. Sequence and stop conditions

1. Design and legal opinion. Assemble lenders, guarantors, notaries, HCSF and ACPR. Map first-security types and legacy covenants. Obtain written positions on in fine treatment, junior-lien recognition, caution consent, usury classification and eligible funding. Cost a loan using actual bank capital and funding assumptions.
2. Bounded, separately priced pilots under existing law. Start where ranking and consent are documentable. Include both same-bank and different-bank cases; record refusals and time spent obtaining existing-loan data. Do not describe use of HCSF flexibility as a scalable solution.
3. Decide the prudential question from evidence. If principal-at-maturity loans cannot satisfy the current rule outside limited flexibility, seek a proposed HCSF amendment with explicit income, consolidated LTV, maturity, exit-plan, stress and reporting conditions. The HCSF may reasonably decline it.
4. Only then standardise distribution. Adopt the voluntary interbank and notarial specification, obtain guarantor participation where viable, and have several banks implement and report against it. Assess a statutory or funding change only for a demonstrated gap.

Reasons to stop: no acceptable HCSF treatment; consent routinely refused or too slow for cautioned first loans; unreliable first-loan balances or valuations; junior-lien capital and funding costs that prevent attractive all-in pricing; inability to demonstrate a credible final repayment without relying on rising house prices or refinancing; or weak customer understanding of the capital still due. The PTZ’s operational success and the rechargeable mortgage’s repeal both argue for measuring these conditions before building a national label.

Research still needed: a sample review of existing mortgage and caution contracts; a French-law opinion against CRR3’s junior-lien enforcement conditions; guarantor eligibility and pricing decisions; loan-purpose-specific usury and covered-bond opinions; bank-by-bank funding quotes; and pilot evidence on borrower demand and principal repayment. Until those results and an HCSF position exist, “standardisable under existing security law” is established; “cheap, interoperable and distributable at scale” remains open.
