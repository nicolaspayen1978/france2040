# HCSF pathway, and three binding constraints

19 October 2026. Working file. Not a public snapshot. Not an épreuve contradictoire. Not a product, and not a plan to persuade anyone.

Opened from the secretariat’s reply of the same day, filed in `Docs/sources/HCSF_secretariat_2026-10-19.md` and published as `/documents/standard-credit-logement` version `2026-10-19`. That reply closed the reading of the current framework. This note does not reopen it. It does not change the 700 Md€ path, EC-05, or the frozen Dutch snapshot `/documents/pays-bas-interets-seuls` version `2026-10-13`.

Two questions, both open.

**A.** If affordability stays a notional amortising burden, who actually gains purchasing power from an interest-only contract? EC-01 version `2026-10-19-2` now states the two income multiples, about 10.6 times on contractual interest and about 5.1 times on a 20-year notional annuity. Household counts under either multiple are empty. So is the headcount of people who stop short of an unchanged ceiling in order to keep cash.

**B.** What evidence and process accompanied the HCSF’s own amendments of the housing-credit decision?

## 1. What has actually revised the decision

The HCSF amends its own decision. Parliament is not the step visible in the texts read here.

Legal basis, as stated in the HCSF’s notification to the ESRB of Decision D-HCSF-2023-2: article L. 631-2-1 (5°) of the Code monétaire et financier. The HCSF’s own description of that article is that it may adopt borrower-based measures to prevent excessive increases in asset prices or excessive indebtedness. Compliance is monitored through ACPR reporting on new housing loans.

Sequence read from the HCSF’s own accounts, not from commentary:

| Practice before 2019 | A debt-service ratio of 33 % was a jurisprudential benchmark among banks, not this decision | ESRB notification of 10 July 2023, section 2.5 |

They say a debt-service limit was chosen over a loan-to-value limit because French banks already judged solvency that way, and because the risk was in new loans rather than in the stock |
| Decision D-HCSF-2023-2 of 29 June 2023 | Technical adjustment inside the same 20 %. The share of the margin reserved for the primary residence goes from at least 80 % to at least 70 %. The first-time-buyer share of the margin stays at least 30 %. The free slice of the margin therefore goes from 20 % to 30 % of the margin, which is 6 % of quarterly production rather than 4 %. A limited breach of an allocation limit in one quarter may be cured over that quarter and the next two. It says the changes do not alter the balance or the scope of the measure |
| Decision D-HCSF-2023-6 of 18 December 2023 | The works threshold that opens a deferral falls from at least 25 % to at least 10 %. A bridge loan is left out of the effort rate when its financing quotient is at most 80 %. In force 1 January 2024, for new credit whose first drawdown is on or after that date | The decision’s own recitals, Journal officiel of 24 December 2023. The 2021 decision is described as having made housing credit safer, and as resting on the effort rate and on a reasonable maturity. The June 2023 decision is described as having removed practical difficulties that the ACPR had observed and reported. Residual practical difficulties remained. The rise in interest rates, market dynamics, and still-high household debt are named. The Governor of the Banque de France proposed the text on 4 December 2023 |

The pattern in these two 2023 decisions is narrow. The ACPR reports a practical difficulty. The Governor proposes a text. The HCSF adopts a technical amendment. The published reason is that the difficulty can be removed without changing the risk judgement, the 35 % test, or the 25-year cap. Neither 2023 decision creates an alternative affordability calculation for an in-fine loan.

That is not a route map for France 2040. It is the standard the next amendment would be read against. A notional amortising burden is not a technical fix of the kind written in 2023. The secretariat said on 19 October 2026 that it would be a modification of the decision. The statutory purpose cited by the HCSF includes excessive asset-price increases as well as excessive debt. A design that raises bids sits inside that purpose. A design that does not raise the regulatory ceiling is a different claim, and it is still untested.

## 2. Three constraints, kept separate

Hypothesis, not a result. Interest-only is not one effect.

An interest-only contract, tested as a notional amortising burden, does not by itself raise the regulatory ceiling. The household may still pay only the contractual interest. Those are different objects. The household exhibit already separates cash retained from income and from wealth (`Docs/Note_Parcours_Menages.md`). This note adds who is bound by which constraint.

1. **Income, at the regulatory ceiling.** The household already wants the maximum the affordability test allows. If that test is a notional annuity, an interest-only contract does not increase the principal the rule permits. It lowers the cash that leaves the account after origination.
2. **Cash flow, by choice.** The household could borrow more under the same test and stops earlier because the amortising payment is more cash than it wants to commit. An interest-only contract can then raise the principal actually taken, up to the same ceiling. That is purchasing power for this group only. In a tight housing market it can still become a higher bid. The parcours note already says an illustrated extra borrowing capacity is not demonstrated purchasing power, and that extra capacity can be capitalised into prices.
3. **Capital.** The deposit, the loan-to-value, or the equity already in the house is what binds. An interest-only contract does little here unless it also changes that constraint.

Acquisition and equity release are not the same case of group 2. A purchase can turn the extra willingness to use the ceiling into a bid. A release of equity from a house already owned has no purchase, and therefore no direct bid, if the proceeds are not themselves used to buy housing. Whether they are is EC-02, and it is open.

The Dutch comparison already filed says the Dutch income test is a notional annuity, not the interest bill, and that a 50 % industry cap and a 100 % consolidated loan-to-value are Dutch parameters that are not imported. Figures quoted elsewhere about a 2026 Dutch monitor — utilisation of 83 % against 92 %, or prices up 21 % against incomes up 14 % — are not in that note. They are not established here.

French averages do not identify group 2. The parcours note records, from the ACPR’s 2025 housing-finance review, a mean effort rate at origination of 30.4 % and a 16.2 % share of loans above 35 %. A mean below the cap is not a count of households who stopped short in order to keep cash.

## 3. What would have to be shown

For question A, the empty cell is the share of French mortgagors who borrow less than the regulatory maximum because the amortising payment exceeds the cash they want to commit. Then, separately, how much of any extra principal taken would bid for a house. Equity release stays a third column, not a row of the purchase table.

For question B, the empty cell is whether a defined interest-only advance, tested on a notional amortising burden, with a cap on the non-amortising slice and a separate consolidated debt-to-value ratio, can be justified against the purpose the HCSF states: borrower solvency, a reasonable maturity, and the risk that looser borrowing capacity becomes higher prices. The 2023 amendments show the procedure. They do not show that this substance would pass it.

The secretariat’s sentence remains the boundary. Implementing a notional burden would require the HCSF to modify its decision. Nothing read here shows that it would.
